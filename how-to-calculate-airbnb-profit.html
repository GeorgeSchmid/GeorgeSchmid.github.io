How to Calculate Airbnb Profit (Step by Step)

Your payout is not your profit. Here's the simple formula, a worked example, and the mistakes that make hosts think they earn more than they do.

The formula

Profit = revenue − platform fees − cost of each stay − fixed costs

Work it out per month. Yearly totals hide the months that lose money, and those are the months where better pricing makes the biggest difference.

Step 1: Add up your revenue

Revenue is everything guests paid for their stays: nightly rate × nights, plus cleaning fees, extra-guest fees and pet fees. Leave out tourist or city taxes you collect for the government, because they aren't yours.

Count each booking in one month only. The simplest rule is to use the check-in date, and to stick to it.

Step 2: Subtract platform fees

In 2026 most Airbnb hosts pay a host-only fee of about 15.5%, taken from the booking total including the cleaning fee. Booking.com commission is typically around 15%, and Vrbo around 8%. Direct bookings have no platform fee, though card payments usually cost a little. The fee calculator shows the exact amount for any booking.

Step 3: Subtract the cost of each stay

Every stay has costs that repeat with each check-in: cleaning, laundry, toiletries, coffee and welcome items, plus wear and tear. Multiply your cost per stay by the number of stays in the month. Short stays mean more turnovers, so a month of 2-night bookings costs more than a month of week-long stays with the same number of nights.

Step 4: Subtract fixed costs

These you pay whether or not anyone books: rent or mortgage, electricity, water, heating, internet, insurance, property taxes and licences, and software such as a channel manager. Annual bills, like insurance, should be spread across the months (divide by 12). The expenses checklist lists everything to include.

The payout from the platform was €1,554.80, yet the profit is €426.80, about a quarter of it. Try your own numbers in the Airbnb profit calculator.

Five mistakes that inflate profit

  1. Treating the payout as income. The payout already has fees removed, but none of your costs.
  2. Forgetting small, frequent costs. Coffee, toilet paper and batteries add up to a real amount over a year.
  3. Ignoring annual bills. Insurance, licences and property tax arrive once but belong to every month.
  4. Not counting repairs and replacements. Towels, bedding, a broken kettle, a repainted wall: set aside a small amount each month.
  5. Looking only at the year. A great summer can hide several loss-making months you could fix with better pricing.

Make it a monthly habit

Once a month, update your bookings and expenses and check four numbers: net profit, occupancy, average nightly rate and profit per property. Ten minutes is enough if everything lives in one place.
